How I Assess the Technology of a Financial Institution — A 7-Layer Framework

Vu Dinh Chung · IT Director, Banking & Financial Services · Updated 2026-11

← Back to study

Most technology assessments start with the stack: core banking, cloud, architecture diagrams, vendor names. After 20+ years building and running technology in Vietnamese banks and consumer finance — and assessing institutions as a partner and a candidate — I have learned that the stack tells you the least.

Here is the framework I use. Seven layers, each scored 1–5. It works for a bank, a finance company, a fintech or a payments business.

Layer 1 — Strategy & governance

Is there a technology roadmap tied to business KPIs? Who owns it — a CIO with a seat at the executive table, or a project office reacting to requests? Is there a real budget cycle (CAPEX and OPEX) that survives contact with the finance committee?

Evidence to ask for: the roadmap, the last budget approval, the last board or exco technology update.

Layer 2 — Architecture

Core banking and product configurability, integration layer (ESB / API gateway), data model, degree of coupling. The test is simple: can the business launch a new product variant through configuration, or does every product change become a code war?

Evidence: time-to-market for the last three product launches.

Layer 3 — Delivery capability

Release cadence, test automation, DevSecOps maturity, the split between in-house and vendors, and the discipline of requirements. Without structured requirements (BPMN/BA practice), delivery speed is an illusion.

Evidence: release history for 12 months, defect escape rate, percentage of automated tests.

Layer 4 — Operations

ITIL processes, uptime and MTTR history, incident patterns, capacity management, monitoring and observability. Anyone can quote an SLA. Few can show the 12-month trend.

Evidence: incident reports, MTTR trend, capacity plan, monitoring dashboards.

Layer 5 — Resilience

DC/DR design, RTO/RPO, and — most importantly — whether a real failover has been executed in the last year. Business continuity plans that live only in a document are a liability, not an asset.

Evidence: the last DR test report and its findings.

Layer 6 — Security & compliance

PCI DSS scope and how it is contained, SBV regulatory compliance (Circulars 09/35 and related), biometric/eKYC controls, DLP/NAC coverage, and the closure rate of audit findings — not just the number of findings.

Evidence: latest audit report, closure tracker, penetration test summary.

Layer 7 — People & partners

Team structure and seniority, key-person risk, vendor and SLA management, knowledge management. In this market the scarce resource is not technology — it is people who can run it.

Evidence: org chart, attrition, vendor contract map, succession plan for critical roles.

Three rules I apply

  • A score below 3 in Layer 1 (strategy) or Layer 6 (security & compliance) caps the overall assessment. Good engineering does not compensate for a missing roadmap or an open compliance gap.
  • Always ask for evidence, not artefacts. Architecture diagrams are aspirational; DR test reports, audit findings and MTTR trends are facts.
  • Weight the layers by business model. A consumer lender, a corporate bank and a payment company should not be scored with the same weights.

How I use it

  • As a leader: to build a 90-day baseline and a prioritised plan that the board can fund.
  • As a candidate: to understand, before accepting a role, where the real work is — and whether the mandate exists to do it.
  • As a partner/advisor: to move a conversation from opinions to evidence.

Bản tiếng Việt (tóm tắt)

Bảy lớp đánh giá: (1) Chiến lược & quản trị; (2) Kiến trúc; (3) Năng lực triển khai; (4) Vận hành; (5) Khả năng chịu lỗi; (6) An ninh & tuân thủ; (7) Con người & đối tác.

Ba nguyên tắc: (1) điểm dưới 3 ở Lớp 1 hoặc Lớp 6 sẽ giới hạn điểm tổng; (2) luôn hỏi bằng chứng, không hỏi sơ đồ; (3) trọng số theo mô hình kinh doanh.

Bảng chấm điểm 1–5 theo từng lớp (có trọng số) — liên hệ nếu bạn muốn bản đầy đủ.
